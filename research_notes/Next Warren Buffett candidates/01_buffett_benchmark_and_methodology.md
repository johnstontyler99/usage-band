# The Buffett Benchmark, the 2026 Succession, a Method for Ranking Would-Be Successors, and the "Next Buffett" Failures

Research date: 2026-11-24. All figures are dated. "Primary" means taken directly from a Berkshire/SEC/DOJ document or the published paper. Figures marked **[secondary]** or **[unverified]** come only from aggregators, search summaries or my own derivations and should be double-checked before publication.

Method note: Berkshire's 2025 annual letter (written by Greg Abel, dated Feb 28, 2026) has the full year-by-year table of Berkshire per-share market value vs the S&P 500 with dividends for 1965-2025. I took the table from the PDF and worked out the decade and trailing-window CAGRs, t-stats and other derived figures myself. These are labelled "computed".

## 1. Buffett's actual compounding record (Berkshire 1965-2025, book value, Buffett Partnership 1957-69, pre-partnership, decade decay, last 10/20 years)

### Takeaway
From 1965 through 2025, Berkshire's per-share market value compounded at **19.7%/yr** against **10.5%/yr** for the S&P 500 with dividends. Cumulatively that is **+6,099,294% vs +46,061%**. Most of the gap was earned before ~1995, though. Over the last 10 years (2016-2025) Berkshire slightly *trailed* the S&P (about 14.3% vs 14.8%/yr, computed). Over 20 years it roughly tied (about 11.3% vs 11.0%). The Buffett Partnership (1957-69) was even better relative to its benchmark: about 29.5% gross and 23.8% net to limited partners, vs 7.4% for the Dow.

### Inferences
- $9,800 to $140,000 over about 6 years implies roughly **56%/yr** (computed). This *overstates* investment skill, because it includes savings from salary (including Graham-Newman). It should be treated as anecdotal, not as a track record.
- Working back from the final BPL figures, 1969 implies about +6% for the partnership and about -11.5% for the Dow (computed). That fits Buffett's "break even" forecast plus the year-end markup of controlled companies.
- The record decays clearly with scale. The gap was about +27pp/yr in 1975-84, +18pp in 1985-94, +3-4pp in 1995-2004, +2pp in 2005-14, and slightly negative in 2015-25. Any "next Buffett" should be compared with the *early* Buffett (BPL and 1965-1995) for return magnitude, and with the *late* Buffett for scale.
- Berkshire's returns are **after corporate-level taxes** and after all costs. Fund records are usually pre-investor-tax, and hedge fund records are often quoted gross. The two are not like-for-like, and the difference flatters funds.

### Gaps
- I could not open the 1970 BPL letter text to confirm the final 1957-69 cumulative figures (commonly cited as partnership +2,794.9% and LP +1,403.5%) from the primary source. Treat the 29.5%/23.8%/7.4% figures as secondary.
- Buffett's own account of his 1951-56 *percentage* returns, separate from the growth in his savings, was not found in a primary source.

## 3. What drove the record ("Buffett's Alpha", float, permanent capital, tax deferral, private vs public, Munger)

### Takeaway
"Buffett's Alpha" (Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen, *FAJ* 2018) found a **Sharpe ratio of 0.79** (market 0.49), **beta 0.69** and **leverage of about 1.7x**. Insurance float financed the leverage at a cost about 3pp *below* T-bills. Once betting-against-beta (BAB) and quality-minus-junk (QMJ) factors are controlled for, Berkshire's alpha falls from 13.4% to 5.4%/yr and becomes statistically insignificant. Put plainly, Buffett levered cheap, safe, high-quality stocks with cheap, permanent, non-callable money, and he survived the drawdowns. A true successor must have (1) a selection edge in value and quality, (2) cheap, stable, non-redeemable leverage or capital, (3) the temperament and structure to sit through 40%+ relative drawdowns, and (4) decades of runway.

### Cited Findings
**Buffett's Alpha: published version (FAJ vol. 74 no. 4, 2018, pp. 35-55; winner of the 2018 Graham and Dodd Award), sample Oct 1976-Mar 2017**
- Berkshire's average excess return over T-bills was **18.6%/yr** (market 7.5%). Volatility was 23.5% (market 15.3%). Sharpe ratio **0.79**, "1.6 times higher than the market's Sharpe ratio of 0.49". Market beta **0.69**. Information ratio **0.64**. $1 invested in Oct 1976 grew to more than **$3,685** by March 2017. — [Frazzini, Kabiller & Pedersen, FAJ 2018 (PDF mirror)](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf); [AQR page](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Buffetts-Alpha)
- Versus other stocks and funds: Berkshire had the highest Sharpe and information ratio of all stocks with a 40-year history (1976-2017), and its Sharpe ranks in the top 3% of all CRSP mutual funds. Only 133 funds survived from 1976 to 2017, which limits the comparison. — [FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Leverage: "we estimated Buffett's average leverage to be **1.7 to 1**" (Table 2: 1.71). Measured another way it is 1.4x. On average, **35%** of Berkshire's liabilities were insurance float. — [FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Cost of float: the estimated average annual cost was **1.72%**, **2.97pp below the T-bill rate**. Float cost was negative in 63% of years. Subperiod spreads vs T-bills ranged from -7.52pp (1976-80) to +1.10pp (1981-85). Berkshire's debt was AAA-rated 1989-2009, and it issued the "first ever negative-coupon security in 2002." Deferred taxes act like an "interest-free loan": $28B of such liabilities in 2011. Berkshire also profited by *selling* embedded leverage (index puts, CDS) with no collateral requirements. — [FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Leverage alone does not explain the record: levering the market 1.7x gives about a 12.7% excess return, "far short" of 18.6%. — [FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Factor regression (Table 4, Berkshire stock, 10/1976-3/2017). Annualized alpha: CAPM **13.4%** (t=4.01). Adding size, value and momentum: 11.0% (t=3.30). Adding BAB: 8.5% (t=2.55). Adding QMJ: **5.4% (t=1.55, insignificant)**. Loadings: MKT 0.95, SMB -0.13, HML 0.40, BAB 0.27, QMJ 0.47; R² 0.29. For the 13F public-stock portfolio, alpha falls from 5.8% (t=3.09) to **0.3% (t=0.16)** after all factors, with BAB 0.15 and QMJ 0.37. — [FAJ 2018, Table 4](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Public vs private: from 1980 to 2017, private companies averaged 65% of holdings, rising from "less than 20% in the early 1980s to more than 78% in 2017." Public stocks had a Sharpe of 0.74 and private holdings 0.45. "The public stocks have performed the best, which suggests that Buffett's returns are more the result of stock selection than of his effect on management." — [FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- A systematic "Buffett-style" portfolio (factor-mimicking, levered to Berkshire's active risk) "performed comparably to Berkshire." The unlevered long-only version had a Sharpe of 0.61 (13F-based). — [FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Drawdown: "from 30 June 1998 to 29 February 2000, Berkshire lost 44% of its market value while the overall stock market was gaining 32%". That is a 76-point relative shortfall that "many fund managers might have had trouble surviving." Buffett survived thanks to his "impeccable reputation and unique structure as a corporation". — [FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Conclusion: Buffett's returns are "neither luck nor magic but, rather, a reward for leveraging cheap, safe, high-quality stocks". His genius lies partly in "applying leverage without ever having a fire sale, and sticking to his principles." The paper quotes Buffett's 1994 line: "it is not necessary to do extraordinary things to get extraordinary results." — [FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)
- Version discrepancy: the 2013 NBER working paper and AQR's abstract page give a **Sharpe of 0.76** and **leverage of about 1.6-to-1**, from an earlier sample. Cite the FAJ 2018 figures (0.79 / 1.7x) as final. — [NBER w19681](https://www.nber.org/papers/w19681); [AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Journal-Article/Buffetts-Alpha)

**Float, permanence, capital structure (Berkshire's own words)**
- Abel (2025 letter) describes Buffett as "building a great insurance business since the acquisition of National Indemnity in 1967, and deploying the float." He calls the equity portfolio "an integral extension of our insurance operations and capital base." Insurance subsidiaries returned $29B of dividends to the parent in 2025. — [2025 Letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2025ltr.pdf)
- Buffett (2014): float is "money that doesn't belong to us but that we can invest for Berkshire's benefit". Because of how the contracts are written, Berkshire "can never be subject to immediate demands for sums that are large compared to our cash resources." Any decline in float would be "no more than 3% in any year." — [2014 Letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2014ltr.pdf)
- Buy-and-hold permanence and tax deferral in practice: Coca-Cola bought for $1.3B now pays $816M/yr in dividends (about 63% yield on cost, computed), and American Express cost $1.3B and is worth $56.1B (2025 letter). Holding "preferably forever" defers capital-gains tax indefinitely. — [2025 Letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2025ltr.pdf)

**Charlie Munger's influence**
- Buffett (2014): Charlie's "blueprint ... was simple: Forget what you know about buying fair businesses at wonderful prices; instead, buy wonderful businesses at fair prices." He added: "Berkshire has been built to Charlie's blueprint." He calls 1972 (See's Candies) "a turning point." — [2014 Letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2014ltr.pdf)
- Buffett (2023): "Charlie was the 'architect' of the present Berkshire." Munger "died on November 28 [2023], just 33 days before his 100th birthday." — [2023 Letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2023ltr.pdf)

### Inferences
- **Share of alpha explained (computed from Table 4):** for Berkshire stock, BAB + QMJ + size/value/momentum remove about **60%** of CAPM alpha (13.4% → 5.4%). For the public-stock book they remove about **95%** (5.8% → 0.3%). The residual for Berkshire stock (5.4%, not significant) plausibly reflects the leverage/float advantage, private deals on special terms, and corporate structure.
- **Successor traits implied:** (a) a disciplined value-plus-quality selection process; (b) a source of *cheap, long-dated, non-redeemable* capital or leverage, such as insurance float, permanent holding-company equity or deferred taxes; (c) structural immunity to redemptions, so that a 44% loss against a +32% market (1998-2000) is survivable; (d) a willingness to concentrate in compounding franchises and hold for decades; (e) a Munger-like intellectual partner, plus an evolution from "cigar butts" to quality; (f) longevity, with roughly 70 years of active compounding.
- Because a systematic factor portfolio "performed comparably," much of Buffett's edge can be replicated *in hindsight*. The rare part was discovering and committing to it early and having the structure to apply leverage without forced selling. Skill judgments about candidates should therefore control for value/quality/low-beta exposure and leverage.

### Gaps
- There is no rigorous study of Berkshire's *tax* advantage (corporate-level taxation vs deferral). Buffett's Alpha notes deferred taxes but does not quantify their contribution.
- The paper's sample ends in March 2017. No updated factor attribution covering 2017-2025 was found.

## 4. Succession status in 2026 (Abel, Buffett's roles, letters, Combs, Weschler, 2026 events)

### Takeaway
Greg Abel became CEO on **Jan 1, 2026** and wrote the 2025 annual letter (Feb 28, 2026). Buffett stayed on as chairman until **Sept 18, 2026**. On that date he became **Chairman Emeritus** (remaining a director), and his son **Howard G. Buffett** was elected Chairman. ## 5. Methodology: how to judge a "Buffett-class" compounder (and a proposed scoring framework)

### Takeaway
Fund-manager outperformance rarely persists. Even Buffett's 61-year record has a t-stat of only about 3.2 on annual excess returns, and his post-1990 excess alone is statistically indistinguishable from zero. A credible ranking must therefore reward **long, audited, net-of-fee, outside-capital records at large scale** and must penalize leverage, concentration-driven luck, survivorship and self-reporting. Below I propose a 100-point framework calibrated so that the Berkshire/BPL record scores in the high 80s to low 90s.

### Cited Findings
**Persistence evidence**
- SPIVA U.S. Persistence Scorecard (year-end 2025): only **2%** of top-quartile small-cap funds stayed top-quartile over five years. *No* mid-cap fund in the top quartile in 2021 was still there at the end of the five-year period. **23%** of fourth-quartile domestic equity funds were merged or liquidated within five years, vs **10%** of top-quartile funds. — [S&P DJI Persistence Scorecard YE2025](https://www.spglobal.com/spdji/en/documents/spiva/persistence-scorecard-year-end-2025.pdf)
- A secondary summary says only **0.46%** of domestic equity funds in the top quartile in 2021 stayed there through December 2025. Of 173 large-cap funds in the top quartile in 2023, 28.9% were still there at end-2025. **[secondary]** — [Advisor Perspectives, May 8 2026](https://www.advisorperspectives.com/commentaries/2026/05/08/s-p-persistence-scorecard-reveals-universal-struggles-for-active-strategies?topic=tax-planning)

**Luck vs skill, survivorship**
- Buffett's Alpha frames the issue as the 1984 Columbia debate. Michael Jensen argued Buffett could be "the happy winner of a coin-flipping contest," and Buffett answered with "Graham-and-Doddsville." The authors note that "selecting successful investors who are informally classified as belonging to Graham-and-Doddsville ex post is subject to biases." The paper's peer set is limited to the 133 funds that survived 1976-2017, which is survivorship-conditioned. — [FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)

**Dollar-gain rankings (LCH Investments)**
- LCH ranks managers by **net dollar gains since inception, net of fees**, usually including the manager's own capital. A firm stays in after the founder's retirement if the approach is unchanged, and gains are frozen once a manager stops taking outside money. Index-linked and closed-end vehicles are excluded. Through Dec 31, 2025 the top 20 had made $969.8B since inception and $115.8B in 2025. That was **41.0%** of all hedge fund gains, from just **16.6%** of assets. — [Hedge Fund Alpha on LCH 2025 survey](https://hedgefundalpha.com/news/top-20-hedge-funds-earn-115b-in-2025/)
- 2025 leaders since inception: Citadel $90.4B (2025: $7.4B), D.E. Shaw $79.9B, Bridgewater $79.1B, TCI $68.4B (2025: a record $18.9B), Soros Fund Management $43.9B (10th). The top 20's money-weighted return in 2025 was 15.7% vs 12.6% for the HFRI composite. Sopher says top managers "carefully control capacity," and most "either limited inflows or returned capital." — [Institutional Investor](https://www.institutionalinvestor.com/article/chris-hohns-tci-tops-hedge-fund-gains-2025); [Hedge Fund Alpha](https://hedgefundalpha.com/news/top-20-hedge-funds-earn-115b-in-2025/)
- Note: one source describes Rick Sopher as chairman of LCH Investments NV "from 2001 to 2025," which suggests he may no longer lead it. — [Hedge Fund Alpha (2024 survey)](https://hedgefundalpha.com/news/great-money-managers-of-2024-lch-investments/)

**Time-weighted vs money-weighted (investor) returns**
- ARK attracted about **$20.6B** of inflows in 2021, after ARKK's +~150% year in 2020, so most investor money arrived near the peak. Morningstar estimated that ARK's ETFs destroyed **$14.3B** of shareholder value over the 10 years to end-2023. — [Yahoo Finance/Reuters on Morningstar](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/cathie-woods-ark-invest-destroyed-223315637.html)

**Size is the anchor (Buffett's and Abel's own words)**
- Buffett (2023 letter): "size did us in, though increased competition for purchases was also a factor." Also: "Doubling our huge base is simply not possible within, say, a five-year period," and "we have no possibility of eye-popping performance." Berkshire "should do a bit better than the average American corporation ... Anything beyond 'slightly better,' though, is wishful thinking." — [2023 Letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2023ltr.pdf); [CNBC, Feb 24 2024](https://www.cnbc.com/2024/02/24/warren-buffett-says-berkshire-may-only-do-slightly-better-than-the-average-company-due-to-its-sheer-size.html)
- Abel (2025 letter): "At Berkshire's scale, the math of compounding works against us." — [2025 Letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2025ltr.pdf)

**Statistical significance of Buffett's own record (computed from the 2025 Letter's annual table; arithmetic annual excess vs S&P TR, simple t-test)** — [source table](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2025ltr.pdf)
- 1965-2025 (n=61): mean excess +11.8pp, sd 28.7, **t ≈ 3.2**
- 1980-2025 (n=46): +7.4pp, t ≈ 2.4
- 1990-2025 (n=36): +2.7pp, **t ≈ 0.9**
- 2000-2025 (n=26): +1.9pp, t ≈ 0.7
- 2005-2025 (n=21): -0.1pp, t ≈ 0.0
- Cross-check: Buffett's Alpha's information ratio of 0.64 over about 40.5 years implies t ≈ 0.64×√40.5 ≈ 4.1, which matches the paper's CAPM alpha t-stat of 4.01. — [FAJ 2018](https://gwern.net/doc/economics/2018-frazzini.pdf)

### Inferences
**Years needed to prove skill (arithmetic: t ≈ IR × √T, so T = (t/IR)²):**

| Information ratio | Years for t=2 | Years for t=3 |
|---|---|---|
| 0.3 (good active manager) | 44 | 100 |
| 0.5 (excellent) | 16 | 36 |
| 0.64 (Buffett 1976-2017) | ~10 | ~22 |
| 1.0 (exceptional) | 4 | 9 |

So a 10-15 year record from even a very good manager cannot statistically separate skill from luck, especially once you account for the thousands of managers being searched (a multiple-testing problem), survivorship, and style-factor exposure.

**Proposed "Buffett-Class Compounder Score" (100 points + penalties).** Each candidate is scored on documented data only. Every input is labelled audited, regulatory-filed (13F/10-K/fund reports) or self-reported.

1. **Duration (15 pts)**: years of continuous record under the same decision-maker. 30+ yrs = 15, 20 = 11, 15 = 8, 10 = 5, <10 = 2. (Buffett: 69 yrs including BPL = 15.)
2. **Excess return, net of fees (20 pts)**: annualized net return minus an *appropriate* benchmark (local total-return index for the strategy; also report vs a style/factor benchmark). +10pp/yr or more = 20; +5 = 12; +3 = 8; +1 = 3. Hedge funds are measured net to LPs (BPL LP 23.8% vs Dow 7.4% = +16pp; Berkshire +9.2pp over 61 yrs).
3. **Statistical confidence (10 pts)**: t-stat of excess return or alpha (IR×√T). t≥3 = 10; t≥2 = 6; t<1.5 = 0. Bonus credit if alpha survives value/quality/low-beta (BAB/QMJ) controls.
4. **Scale (15 pts)**: peak capital managed and cumulative dollar value added (LCH-style net dollar gains). $100B+ value added = 15; $10B+ = 10; $1B+ = 5; below that = 1. This is where Buffett is unmatched. Small-capital records (personal IRAs, sub-$1B funds) cannot score highly.
5. **Risk and leverage-adjusted quality (10 pts)**: Sharpe/IR, maximum drawdown and its recovery, and explicit leverage. Berkshire: Sharpe 0.79, beta 0.69, about 1.7x leverage, but with cheap non-callable float; drawdowns of -48.7% (1974), -44% (1998-2000) and -31.8% (2008). Margin-, swap- or repo-funded leverage counts against a manager; long-dated, non-callable funding does not.
6. **Verification (10 pts)**: audited and public (listed company, SEC-registered fund) = 10; LP-reported with audited statements = 7; press-reported or self-reported only = 2.
7. **Outside-capital, investor-realized results (10 pts)**: open to outside investors, with money-weighted returns close to time-weighted returns (no ARK-style inflow-at-peak destruction); fees reasonable (BPL: no GP allocation below a 6% hurdle).
8. **Persistence and post-fame performance (10 pts)**: outperformance across sub-periods and regimes, and *after* the manager became famous or large (the window where Miller, Berkowitz, Woodford and Douglass broke down).

**Red-flag penalties (subtract up to 20):** a single position above 25-30% of assets (Sequoia/Valeant, Fairholme/AIG-St. Joe-Sears, ESL/Sears); a liquidity mismatch (Woodford); undisclosed or derivative leverage (Archegos); key-person or governance risk with no succession; performance concentrated in one boom year; records that cannot be independently replicated.

**Calibration.** On this rubric the Berkshire/BPL record scores in the high 80s to low 90s. It loses points mainly on post-1995 persistence and on the factor-adjusted significance of its public-stock alpha. Most famous candidates will score 40-70, chiefly because of short duration, small scale or unverified records. Report results as "score + confidence band" rather than a precise rank.

**Benchmark choice:** compare US large-cap value managers with the S&P 500 TR *and* a value/quality factor benchmark. Compare international managers with local TR indices in local currency and USD. Compare holding-company compounders (Markel, Fairfax, Constellation, etc.) on per-share market value *and* per-share book/intrinsic value, after corporate taxes, which is the same basis as Berkshire.

- I did not retrieve the primary text of the academic luck-vs-skill literature (e.g., Fama-French 2010 "Luck versus Skill in the Cross-Section of Mutual Fund Returns"; Carhart 1997). The required-years table above is arithmetic, not a cited study.

## 6. Cautionary tales: people anointed "the next Buffett" who failed or faded

### Takeaway
The recurring failure pattern is this: an excellent 10-15 year record earned on modest capital earns a "next Buffett" label, which brings a flood of assets. The manager then responds with concentration in one or two names, leverage, or illiquid holdings, and redeemable capital turns a bad bet into a collapse. Buffett avoided every one of these traps through permanent capital, cheap non-callable float, and a refusal to let one position or one lender threaten the enterprise.

### Cited Findings
**Eddie Lampert (ESL Investments / Sears)**
- *Peak:* BusinessWeek's 2004 cover asked whether he was "The Next Warren Buffett." ESL averaged about **29%/yr from 1988 to 2004** (other estimates: about 24.5% for 1988-2002). — [BusinessWeek cover story (Scribd)](https://www.scribd.com/document/355817248/The-Next-Warren-Buffett-Businessweek); [Yahoo Finance](https://finance.yahoo.com/news/invest-eddie-lampert-183000299.html)
- *Collapse:* Sears Holdings filed for Chapter 11 in **October 2018**. Investors left ESL "in droves" over its concentration in Sears. One estimate says fund value fell to about **$512M** from $3.5B (2013). **[secondary]** — [Wikipedia: ESL Investments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ESL_Investments); [Wikipedia: Eddie Lampert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddie_Lampert)
- *Lesson:* a hedge fund manager who becomes the CEO of a declining retailer and concentrates the fund in it is the opposite of a Buffett-style decentralized, diversified collection of businesses. Financial engineering cannot fix a business losing its moat.

**Bill Miller (Legg Mason Value Trust)**
- *Peak:* beat the S&P 500 for **15 straight years** (1991-2005). Assets grew from about $650M to close to $20B by 2005. The fund was a "$16.5 billion mutual fund" in March 2008. — [Fortune/CNN Money, Mar 2008](https://money.cnn.com/2008/03/05/news/newsmakers/lashinsky_miller.fortune/); [InvestmentNews](https://www.investmentnews.com/ria-news/as-the-bill-miller-era-ends-how-will-legg-mason-move-beyond-his-legacy/68835)
- *Collapse:* the streak ended in 2006. The fund then fell **about 55% in 2008** vs -37% for the S&P 500 (with heavy financials such as Countrywide). It "never recaptured its old glory." — [Baltimore Sun](https://baltimoresun.com/business/bs-xpm-2013-06-29-bs-bz-peters-20130629-story.html); [Motley Fool, 2008](https://www.fool.com/investing/mutual-funds/2008/07/21/was-bill-miller-just-lucky.aspx)
- *Lesson:* a calendar-year "streak" is a weak statistic (sensitive to year-end dates). Averaging down into leveraged financials without a balance-sheet-risk filter is ruinous. Persistence after fame is the real test.

**Bruce Berkowitz (Fairholme Fund)**
- *Peak:* Morningstar's **Domestic-Stock Fund Manager of the Decade** (named Jan 2010), with 10-year returns through 2009 of **13.2%/yr** vs a category average of 0.01%. — [Morningstar via PR Newswire](https://www.prnewswire.com/news-releases/morningstar-names-bruce-berkowitz-david-herro-and-bill-gross-fund-managers-of-the-decade-81226247.html)
- *Collapse:* lost **32% in 2011** while the S&P 500 gained 2.1% (concentrated in AIG, St. Joe and financials). Assets fell from a 2011 peak of about **$20B** to $2.2B by 2017. He later bet on Sears. — [Forbes, Feb 2013](https://www.forbes.com/sites/abrambrown/2013/02/04/a-reminder-of-the-celebrity-and-controversy-around-star-investor-bruce-berkowitz/); [CNBC, Jun 2017](https://www.cnbc.com/2017/06/29/fairholme-funds-berkowitz--abysmal-performance-this-decade.html)
- *Lesson:* "manager of the decade" awards come at peak inflows. Extreme concentration combined with redeemable mutual fund capital forces the manager to sell at the bottom.

**Neil Woodford (UK)**
- *Peak:* about 25 years at Invesco Perpetual (1988-2014), running the Income (£10.36bn) and High Income (£13.64bn) funds. Called "Britain's best fund manager" and, in the Telegraph's words, "Britain's Buffett." Raised a "record £1.6 billion" at launch in 2014. — [Wikipedia: Neil Woodford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Woodford)
- *Collapse:* the Woodford Equity Income Fund was **suspended on June 4, 2019** after assets fell from about £10.2bn at peak to about £3.7bn. Kent County Council's £250m redemption was the trigger. The fund was wound up and Woodford removed in **October 2019**, with a -35.9% three-year return. In April 2023 Link agreed to pay up to £235m (about 77p in the pound). In **August 2025** the FCA fined Woodford nearly £6m and his firm £40m and banned him; both appealed, so the findings are provisional. — [Which?](https://www.which.co.uk/news/article/neil-woodfords-3-7bn-fund-suspended-will-investors-lose-out-aSWEr8h2nKEx); [CNBC, Oct 2019](https://www.cnbc.com/2019/10/15/star-uk-fund-manager-woodford-forced-to-close-embattled-flagship-fund.html); [Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Woodford)
- *Lesson:* a liquidity mismatch (unquoted and AIM stocks inside a daily-dealing fund) is fatal. The fund was more than 20% in small AIM companies. A record built in a large-cap UK income style did not carry over to venture-style holdings.

**Hamish Douglass (Magellan, Australia)**
- *Peak:* co-founder of Magellan, Australia's leading global equity manager and a local "Buffett" figure. **[label not confirmed in sources found]**
- *Collapse:* CEO Brett Cairns resigned in December 2021, and Douglass left the board in March 2022. Funds under management roughly **halved during 2022 to A$45.3B** (about A$50B drained in 12 months) amid leadership turmoil and global-strategy underperformance. FUM was A$39.9B at Dec 31, 2025. — [Business News Australia](https://www.businessnewsaustralia.com/articles/magellan-funds-drained-of--50-billion-over-12-months.html); [Wikipedia: Magellan Financial Group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magellan_Financial_Group)
- *Lesson:* key-person risk and macro-thematic bets (the specific China-tech and other calls were not confirmed in fetched sources) can unwind a listed asset manager quickly. An asset manager's own franchise is not "permanent capital."

**Sequoia Fund / Valeant (Ruane, Cunniff & Goldfarb)**
- *Peak:* Sequoia was the fund Buffett recommended to his partners when BPL closed, an exemplar of the Buffett lineage. **[context: commonly cited; not confirmed in the sources fetched here]**
- *Collapse:* Valeant reached about **30-32% of net assets** at peak and cost about **6.3pp of the fund's -7.3% 2015 return**. Robert Goldfarb retired in **March 2016**, and David Poppe took over the $5.6B fund. The stake was fully sold by mid-June 2016, and shareholders sued. — [Fortune, Mar 2016](https://fortune.com/2016/03/24/sequoia-chief-resigns-valeant/); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2016-03-23/goldfarb-to-retire-from-sequoia-fund-after-valeant-losses); [Fortune, Jul 2016](https://fortune.com/2016/07/13/valeant-sequoia-fund/); [Kiplinger](https://www.kiplinger.com/article/investing/T041-C009-S002-sequoia-fund-tries-to-recover.html)
- *Lesson:* even a Buffett-endorsed shop failed by letting a position grow to about a third of the fund in a serial acquirer with aggressive accounting. Position limits matter, and so does avoiding "roll-up" businesses that fail Buffett's quality and integrity tests.

**Cathie Wood (ARK Invest)**
- *Peak:* ARKK returned about **170% in 2020** (other sources: about 150%) and became the largest actively managed ETF at $17B (Dec 2020). — [Wikipedia: ARK Invest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARK_Invest)
- *Collapse:* by Dec 9, 2022 ARKK was **-78% from its February 2021 peak** and -63% for 2022. Morningstar estimated ARK's ETFs destroyed **$14.3B** of shareholder value in the 10 years to 2023, with ARKK third-worst individually at $7.1B. ARK's 2021 inflows were $20.6B. Later rebound: ARKK was the fifth-best ETF of 2025 per Morningstar. — [Wikipedia: ARK Invest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARK_Invest); [Yahoo/Reuters on Morningstar](https://ca.finance.yahoo.com/news/cathie-woods-ark-invest-destroyed-223315637.html)
- *Lesson:* time-weighted returns can look fine while investors' money-weighted returns are disastrous. High-beta, unprofitable-growth exposure is the opposite of Buffett's low-beta and quality factor loadings.

**Masayoshi Son (SoftBank)**
- *Peak:* invested **$20M in Alibaba (2000)**, a stake worth about **$75B by 2014** and SoftBank's 29.5% stake worth about $108.7B (Oct 2018). — [Wikipedia: Masayoshi Son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masayoshi_Son)
- *Collapse:* lost "more than **$59 billion**" in the 2000 dot-com crash (often cited as about $70B). Launched the **$100B Vision Fund**. Record **¥3.5 trillion (~$27.4B)** loss for the year to March 2022. WeWork was a major loss. **[secondary]** — [Wikipedia: Masayoshi Son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masayoshi_Son)
- *Lesson:* one 1,000x hit plus leverage and momentum in venture assets produces extreme boom-bust paths. Without margin of safety and low leverage, the long-run CAGR is volatile and does not compound in a Buffett-like way.

**Bill Hwang (Archegos)**
- *Peak:* a Tiger Management alumnus whose family office reached about **$36B** of exposure/value before 2021. — [Bloomberg, Nov 20 2024](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/articles/2024-11-20/archegos-founder-bill-hwang-sentenced-to-18-years-in-prison)
- *Collapse:* collapsed in March 2021 on margin calls on concentrated total-return-swap positions. Banks lost **more than $10B** (DOJ/judge: over $9B, with Credit Suisse hardest hit at $5.5B). Hwang was convicted in July 2024 on 10 counts and **sentenced to 18 years** on Nov 20, 2024. He is reportedly free on bail pending appeal (as of March 2026, per Wikipedia). — [DOJ SDNY](https://www.justice.gov/usao-sdny/pr/founder-and-head-archegos-capital-management-bill-hwang-sentenced-18-years-prison); [NBC News](https://www.nbcnews.com/news/us-news/archegos-bill-hwang-sentenced-18-years-prison-massive-us-fraud-rcna181134); [Wikipedia: Bill Hwang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Hwang)
- *Lesson:* hidden, callable leverage combined with concentration ends in ruin. This is the mirror image of Buffett's non-callable float ("never ... subject to immediate demands for sums that are large compared to our cash resources" — [2014 Letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2014ltr.pdf)).

- *Lesson (inferred):* copying BPL's fee structure and Buffett's style is not the same as replicating Buffett's results. Judge on audited, net, long-horizon numbers.

### Inferences
- **The common failure modes, ranked by frequency in these cases:** (1) concentration in one or two names (Lampert/Sears, Berkowitz/AIG-St. Joe-Sears, Sequoia/Valeant, Hwang); (2) redeemable or short-term capital combined with illiquidity (Woodford, Fairholme, Magellan, Miller); (3) leverage that can be called (Hwang, Son); (4) a fame-driven flood of assets near the peak (Miller, Berkowitz, ARK, Woodford); (5) style drift after success (Woodford into unquoted stocks, Lampert into operating Sears).
- **Implication for the scoring framework:** weight "post-fame persistence" and "capital structure" heavily. Apply hard penalties for positions above 25-30% and for callable leverage. A candidate's best decade should never be the only decade scored.
