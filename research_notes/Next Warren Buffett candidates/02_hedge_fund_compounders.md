# Hedge fund, multi-strategy, macro, quant and activist compounders vs. Warren Buffett

Research date: 2026-11-24. Buffett yardstick (from the assignment brief, not re-verified here): Berkshire about 20%/yr for about 60 years vs about 10%/yr for the S&P 500. That is roughly 56,000x vs roughly 300x cumulative (my arithmetic: 1.20^60 and 1.10^60).

**Source-quality key used below:**
- **[A]** Audited, regulatory or primary: annual report, investor letter, Form ADV, rating-agency report.
- **[B]** Reputable press citing people familiar or firm data: Bloomberg, Reuters, FT, WSJ, Institutional Investor (II), CNBC, Forbes.
- **[C]** Book or historical secondary: Wikipedia citing a book or press.
- **[D]** Aggregator, blog or search-snippet only. Treat as an unverified lead.

## Q1. Who tops LCH Investments' "Top 20 managers by net gains since inception" (Jan 2025 and Jan 2026 editions)?

### Takeaway
Ken Griffin's Citadel is No. 1 in both recent editions:
- About $83B net gains since 1990 as of end-2024 (Jan 2025 edition).
- $90.4B as of end-2025 (Jan 2026 edition).

D.E. Shaw is No. 2 in both. Ranks 3–5 at end-2025 were Bridgewater, Millennium and TCI. TCI made a record $18.9B in 2025, the largest one-year dollar gain ever recorded.

LCH ranks **cumulative dollar gains net of fees**, not CAGR. It therefore rewards scale and longevity. It excludes Renaissance's Medallion fund, and it freezes managers once they stop running outside money (Soros, Moore).

**Methodology, and why Buffett-style compounders are missing from it**
- Figures are net of fees and include founders' own capital in the firm's vehicles.
- Gains are frozen once a manager stops managing outside money. Example: Moore was removed in the Jan 2024 edition after returning outside capital.
- Index-linked and closed-end vehicles such as CLOs are excluded.
- Sources: [HedgeFundAlpha on LCH, 21 Jan 2024](https://hedgefundalpha.com/investor-letters/great-money-managers-lch-investments/)

### Inferences
- Citadel's lead has widened from $28.6B (end-2017) to $90.4B (end-2025). That is about $62B of net gains in eight years, despite Citadel routinely handing back profits to cap AUM.
- TCI's rise from 14th to 5th in three years is the fastest climb in recent editions.
- At TCI's current pace of about $13B/yr over 2023–25, it would pass Millennium's $73.4B in 2026 if returns held. That is speculative.
- Because LCH measures dollars, not rates, it is not a Buffett-comparable list. Several of the highest-CAGR records are invisible or frozen in it:
  - Medallion is excluded.
  - Druckenmiller, Soros and Tiger stopped accruing once they stopped running outside money.
  - Appaloosa now runs mostly Tepper's own money.

## Q2. Detailed records of individual managers

### Takeaway
Very few managers have both very long records and high compounding.

The strongest documented long-duration records are:
- **Medallion** (Renaissance): about 39%/yr net, 1988–2018.
- **Druckenmiller** (Duquesne): about 30%/yr with no losing year, about 1986–2010.
- **Tiger** (Robertson): 31.7% net, 1980–98.
- **Soros Quantum**: about 30%/yr, about 1970–2000.
- **Appaloosa** (Tepper): about 25% net, 1993–2019.
- **Steinhardt**: 24.5% net, 1967–95.
- **Caxton** (Kovner): 21% net, 1983–2011.
- **Citadel Wellington**: 19.19% net, 1990–2025.
- **TCI**: about 18% net, 2004–2025.
- **Pershing Square**: 16.2% net NAV, 2004–2025.

Of these, only **Citadel** (35 years, still running about $65–77B, still open) and **TCI** (22 years, about $57–77B) combine Buffett-like duration, a 15%+ net CAGR, today's scale and continued outside money. Every other top record:
- ended or became a family office (Druckenmiller, Soros, Tiger, Steinhardt, Kovner, SAC/Point72 for 2014–18, Paulson), or
- is capacity-capped and closed (Medallion), or
- has decayed toward or below the S&P 500 (Baupost since 2014, PSH since its 2012 IPO, Bridgewater Pure Alpha since 2005, Elliott, Third Point).

## Q3. Which records show Buffett-like features, and which are high-return but short, small, leveraged or unverifiable?

### Takeaway
On the criteria of 25+ years, 15%+ net, $10B+ scale, modest leverage and few losing years, **Citadel Wellington** is the only *currently active, outside-capital* hedge fund that clearly passes all the numeric tests (35 yrs, 19.19% net, about $65–77B). The caveats are that it uses leverage and that its 2008 drawdown was severe (unverified here).

**Medallion** beats everyone on returns, but it is small relative to its profits (about $10B cap), leveraged, closed to outsiders and not audited publicly.

**Druckenmiller, Soros, Robertson, Steinhardt and Kovner** have Buffett-class rates over 20–30 years, but all stopped compounding outside money.

**TCI** and **Pershing Square** are the strongest "next-Buffett-style" concentrated compounders still running. Both are 22 years old; TCI is at about 18% net, PSH at 16.2% NAV. Both had deep drawdowns (TCI -43% in 2008). Ackman is explicitly building a Berkshire clone at Howard Hughes.

### Inferences
- **Closest overall analogue to Buffett: Citadel.** Duration, CAGR and scale all fit. It differs from Berkshire in using fund leverage and charging pass-through fees rather than compounding permanent capital, and its volatility in 2008 is unverified here.
- **Closest "style" analogue: TCI.** It is concentrated and long-only in spirit, holds a small number of quality franchises, is long-lockup and has six staff. It is about 22 years old vs Buffett's 60. Its -43% in 2008 is far worse than Berkshire's 2008 book-value dip.
- **Most explicit Buffett imitation: Ackman.** He is building HHH plus the Vantage insurer to get float, a permanent-capital structure. But PSH's record since 2012 trails the S&P, and the HHH structure only became operational in 2025–26, so it has no record yet.
- **Druckenmiller** may have the best combination of rate (about 30%) and consistency (no losing year in about 25–30 years) of any discretionary manager. It ran at a modest scale ($12B peak) and ended in 2010, and it is unaudited.
- **Medallion** is in a class of its own on return and Sharpe. It is not "Buffett-like" in scalability: it is capped at about $10B and returns profits to employees, versus Berkshire's $1T-scale.

### Gaps
- No source quantifies leverage (gross/net exposure) for Citadel, Millennium, D.E. Shaw, TCI or Elliott. KBRA describes the methodology only.
- No public max-drawdown data was found for most managers. Known worst years: TCI -43% (2008); Odey about -50% (2016); Tiger Global about -52% (H1 2022); D1 -23% YTD (May 2022).

## Q4. Skill vs. luck and risk-adjusted analyses (Sharpe ratios)

### Takeaway
The only rigorous, published risk-adjusted analysis found this session is for Medallion. Using Zuckerman's 1988–2018 data, it shows:
- 66.1% arithmetic mean and 31.7% standard deviation.
- A Sharpe ratio above 2.0.
- Market beta of about -1.0.
- No significant Fama-French loadings.
- Gross wealth beating even monthly perfect-foresight market timing.

The author calls it a "Michelson-Morley level challenge" to market efficiency.

For the other managers, no audited Sharpe ratios were found. LCH data shows that the top 20 outperform the average manager but take a large share of gross gains as fees.

### Cited Findings
- Medallion statistics from Zuckerman's 1988–2018 data — [Cornell Capital](https://www.cornell-capital.com/blog/2020/02/medallion-fund-the-ultimate-counterexample.html):

  - Sharpe above 2.0.
  - No negative year 1988–2018, by that table's annual figures.
  - Beta about -1.0 against the CRSP market; SMB and HML loadings negative and insignificant.
  - $100 grows to $398.7M gross, vs $1,910 for the market and $331,288 for *monthly perfect-foresight* timing.
  - The fund scaled from about $20M to $10B without visible decay.

  - Tiger: 4 in 21 — [Wikipedia – Robertson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Robertson)

### Inferences
- With a Sharpe above 2 sustained over 30 years, Medallion's record is statistically very hard to attribute to luck. The t-stat on the mean is roughly 66.1/(31.7/√31) ≈ 11.6 (my arithmetic).
- By the same crude test, a manager with about 19% excess return at, say, 15–20% volatility over 35 years (Citadel-like) would also have t > 5. That is an assumed volatility, not sourced. Long-run records of 25+ years at high CAGR are therefore unlikely to be luck. The bigger risks to the comparison are survivorship (Tiger, Odey, Paulson show how records end) and data quality (unaudited, gross vs net mix-ups).
- The fee data suggests that, gross of fees, the top managers' skill is larger than their net numbers show. For an outside investor, though, only net matters, and LCH's own fund-of-funds roughly matched equities.

### Gaps
- No Sharpe ratios or volatility data were found for Citadel, Millennium, D.E. Shaw, TCI, Elliott, Druckenmiller, Soros, Tiger, Appaloosa or Pershing.
- No academic "skill vs luck" study was located this session for these hedge fund managers. Leads to check:
  - Frazzini, Kabiller and Pedersen, "Buffett's Alpha" (2013/2018), with a Berkshire Sharpe of about 0.76 (background knowledge, unverified here).
  - Fung, Hsieh et al. on hedge-fund persistence.
  - Kosowski, Naik and Teo (2007), bootstrap of hedge-fund alpha.
