# Holding-company builders and operator-capital allocators ("mini-Berkshires") vs. Buffett's record

Research date: 2026-11-24. All figures dated. Conventions used below:
- **TSR** = share price plus reinvested dividends (stock return). **BVPS / NAV / IV** = book value, net asset value or company-calculated intrinsic value per share (not a stock return). **IRR** (money-weighted, used by PE funds) is not comparable to a per-share **CAGR** (time-weighted).
- "totalrealreturns" figures are third-party calculations (dividends reinvested, nominal, US-listed tickers, end date 2026-11-23). That site truncates older US histories to start in 1993 (data limit), so they understate full-tenure records for HEICO, Markel, Loews, etc. Rows fetched together share a common start date.
- Where I computed a CAGR or multiple myself from cited inputs, it is marked "(computed)".

## Key Question 1: Candidate records — person, vehicle, tenure, TSR and/or BVPS/IV CAGR, benchmark, multiple, scale, worst drawdown, capital-allocation method, 2026 status, source quality

### Takeaway
Only a handful of records match Berkshire's ~20%/yr *and* run for 30–40+ years: Fairfax (Watsa, 19.5% stock / 18.7% BVPS since 1985), Danaher (Rales, ~20%/yr since 1984), Brookfield (19% since 1993; Flatt CEO since 2002), HEICO (Mendelsons, ~22% since 1990), plus historical Teledyne, Capital Cities, TCI and Leucadia. Constellation Software (Leonard) has the highest return of any large, long record (~29% a year on price alone since its 2006 IPO, even after a 2025–26 fall of roughly 50%). But Leonard resigned for health reasons in Sept 2025 and left the board in May 2026. Most other "mini-Berkshires" (Markel, Loews, White Mountains, Exor, Kinnevik, Soul Patts, Howard Hughes) compound at roughly 8–16%, which beats their index but is not at Buffett's level.

### Cited Findings

#### Benchmark: Buffett / Berkshire and Greg Abel
- Berkshire's per-share market value rose at a 19.7% compound annual rate from 1965 to 2025, against 10.5% for the S&P 500 with dividends. The overall gain from 1964 to 2025 was 6,099,294%, against 46,061% for the S&P. In 2025 Berkshire returned 10.9% against 17.9% for the S&P. — [Berkshire 2025 Annual Letter (Abel), performance table](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2025ltr.pdf)
- Berkshire's book value per share grew at 18.1% a year over 1965–2025, against a 10.5% annualized return for the S&P 500 TR. Over the past decade BVPS grew 12.4% a year, trailing the index's 14.8%. — [Morningstar, Abel's debut letter](https://www.morningstar.com/stocks/berkshire-hathaway-greg-abel-goes-conservative-with-his-debut-annual-letter-shareholders)
- Abel's first letter was published Feb 28, 2026. Berkshire held $373.3bn of cash, and Abel said this was not a sign Berkshire is uninterested in new investments. — [Fortune, Mar 2, 2026](https://fortune.com/2026/03/02/berkshires-greg-abel-admits-warren-is-obviously-a-very-hard-act-to-follow-in-first-letter-to-shareholders/)
- Greg Abel was born June 1, 1962 (age 64). He joined CalEnergy in 1992, which bought MidAmerican in 1999, and Berkshire took control that year. He became MidAmerican CEO in 2008 (renamed BHE in 2014) and Berkshire CEO from January 2026. He sold his 1% BHE stake for $870m in June 2022. — [Wikipedia: Greg Abel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greg_Abel)
- BHE historically generated 10–12% of Berkshire's operating profit. PacifiCorp had $2.4bn of projected wildfire losses at end-2023 that "could grow to $8bn", and fire victims claim $30bn. Buffett admitted "we made some mistakes" on PacifiCorp. — [Globe and Mail](https://theglobeandmail.com/business/industry-news/energy-and-resources/article-warren-buffetts-pacificorp-utility-singed-by-wildfires/); [AOL/Reuters](https://www.aol.com/news/berkshires-abel-says-wildfires-prompted-180858401.html)
- In 2025 BHE produced $8.4bn of net cash flow from operating activities, "consistent with its five-year average". The letter frames BHE as "rebalancing" and pursuing growth "selectively", and links future investment to the "regulatory compact". — [Berkshire 2025 letter](https://www.berkshirehathaway.com/letters/2025ltr.pdf)
- BRK-A TSR from Jan 1993 to Nov 23, 2026 was 13.44% a year (71x), against 10.58% for SPY, with a max drawdown of −51.47% (Dec 2007 → Mar 2009). — [totalrealreturns](https://totalrealreturns.com/n/MKL,L,WTM,BRK-A,BN,SPY)
- Berkshire B was up about 1.0% YTD as of Sept 19, 2026. — [24/7 Wall St](https://247wallst.com/investing/2026/09/19/is-markel-the-new-berkshire-hathaway-blueprint-for-value-investors-post-buffett/)

It IPO'd in 2006, has acquired "over 850 businesses" per the lead (another section says "over 500"), and spun off Total Specific Solutions to Topicus.com in January 2021. — [Wikipedia: Constellation Software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation_Software)

## Key Question 2: Endowments and institutional allocators often compared to Buffett

### Takeaway
David Swensen's Yale record (13.7% a year over 36 years, 3.4 points a year above the average endowment) is the best-documented institutional record. It is not comparable to Buffett's per-share compounding: it is a diversified, fee-paying, time-weighted portfolio return (much of it via external managers), and ~5% a year is spent. As a result the endowment's dollars grew only ~32x while the return-compounding factor was ~100x.

### Cited Findings
- Over Swensen's 36 years (1985–2021), Yale "produced an annualized gain of 13.7% per annum, outperforming the average endowment (as measured by Cambridge Associates) by 3.4% per annum". The endowment went from about $1.3bn to $42.3bn (June 30, 2021), FY2021 returned 40.2%, and outperformance equaled "over $50 billion in value added" vs the average endowment. — [Yale News, "David Swensen's coda", Oct 22, 2021](https://news.yale.edu/2021/10/22/david-swensens-coda)
- Other periods: 14.4% a year for 1985–2015 and 12.4% average over the 30 years to 2020 (secondary). — [Wikipedia: Yale endowment](https://Www.Wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_Endowment); [Wikipedia: David F. Swensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_F._Swensen). Morningstar called him "the Peter Lynch of institutional investing". — [Morningstar](https://www.morningstar.com/columns/rekenthaler-report/david-swensen-peter-lynch-institutional-investing)
- (Computed) 1.137^36 ≈ 102x compounding factor against a ~32.5x rise in endowment dollars ($1.3bn → $42.3bn), reflecting spending distributions net of gifts.

### Inferences
- Endowment returns are portfolio-level, net of external manager fees, and include illiquid private assets marked by appraisal (smoothed volatility). They are not concentrated operating or per-share records, so the "Buffett of institutional investing" label refers to influence and risk-adjusted excess return, not to a comparable compounding rate. At 13.7% vs Berkshire's ~20% over a similar era, Yale's absolute return is well below Buffett's.
- Endowment and PE returns are often reported as IRRs or time-weighted fiscal-year returns (June year-end). Comparisons to calendar-year per-share CAGRs should note the measurement difference.

### Gaps
- MIT, Bowdoin (Paula Volent), Princeton, Norway's GPFG, GIC and ADIA annualized returns were **not retrieved** because the search budget ran out. No figures are given for them; the report writer should not assume any.

## Key Question 3: Which living, still-active allocator is most Buffett-like on length × return × scale, and who is young enough to compound for decades more?

### Takeaway
On length × return × scale, among living and still-active allocators, **Prem Watsa** (40 years, 19.5% stock / 18.7% BVPS, ~US$26bn of book and ~C$55bn market cap at YE2025) and **Bruce Flatt** (CEO since 2002, ~20% a year to 2022 and 19% for BN over 32 years, $103bn market cap and $1.18tn AUM, age 61) stand out. Flatt is the strongest "young enough" candidate. Mark Leonard had the best return record but is no longer active (health, Sept 2025; off the board May 2026). Younger operators of inherited machines with possible long runways include HEICO's Mendelson brothers, TransDigm's Mike Lisman, Constellation's Mark Miller, Brookfield's Connor Teskey and Kinsale's Michael Kehoe (35% a year, but only a 10-year record).

### Cited Findings
- Watsa: age 76 (b. ### Inferences
- **Most Buffett-like living record (length × return):** Watsa. He has 40 years at ~19.5% (vs Buffett's 61 years at 19.7%) and is still in charge. His weaknesses are volatility (a 1999–2002 collapse and a flat 2011–2020 decade) and age 76.
- **Best combination of record, scale and runway:** Flatt. He has ~24 years as CEO at ~19–20% and runs a $100bn+ market-cap, $1tn+ AUM platform with insurance float (Brookfield Wealth Solutions) that structurally resembles Berkshire. At 61 he could plausibly have 15–20 more years. The caveat is that Brookfield's returns are levered and fee-driven, with a −72% drawdown in 2008–09.
- **Highest returns, but the person is out:** Leonard and Constellation (~29–37%). Constellation's future is now an institutional rather than personal record, and its 2026 crash and 2% organic growth show the risks.
- **Institution over individual:** Danaher, Bergman & Beving/Addtech/Lagercrantz, HEICO and TransDigm show that a codified system plus family or board-level capital-allocation discipline can carry Buffett-level returns across CEO generations. In that sense the "next Buffett" may be a culture, not a person.
- **Young, short records with promise:** Kehoe (Kinsale), Lisman (TransDigm), Miller (Constellation), Teskey (Brookfield) and Elkann (Exor, though NAV has lagged since 2024). None has yet proven a 20-year per-share record in their own right.
- Ackman's Howard Hughes is a 2025–26 structural experiment (insurance float plus a fee-free Pershing portfolio). Its historical HHH TSR of 5.5% a year gives no evidence yet.

### Gaps
- No ages were verified for Mark Leonard, Michael Kehoe, the Mendelson brothers, Mike Lisman, Connor Teskey, Mark Miller or John Elkann. "Young enough" rankings for them rest on tenure, not verified age.
- No independent intrinsic-value series exists for Brookfield or Constellation, so the TSR-based comparisons may be affected by start and end valuation multiples (e.g., Constellation's 2025 peak vs its 2026 trough).
